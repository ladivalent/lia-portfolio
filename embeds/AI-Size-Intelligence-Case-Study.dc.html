01 · The problem
Signal Systems thinking

One tag. Three different bodies.

Picture a giant global closet. Sellers from America, England, and France all toss clothes in. You want a dress. The tag says size 12. Easy, right?

Not quite. A US 12, a UK 12, and a European size are three very different fits. And eBay stored size as plain text typed by each seller, so the platform honestly didn't know which "12" you were looking at. Every purchase was a coin flip.

"Size 12" = US 12 · roomy or UK 12 · a US 8 or EU 40 · same as UK 12 Same number. Three different fits. The seller knew which one they meant. The platform didn't.
41%
of apparel & footwear returns said "doesn't fit", the #1 return reason platform-wide
$400M
spent every year shipping wrong sizes back and forth
2.3×
higher churn for new buyers after one bad-fit purchase

This wasn't a UI bug. It was a structural seam in the marketplace: size went in as free text and came out as free text. Nobody translated in the middle. That's where the design work had to happen.

02 · My challenge
Signal Framing the real problem

Help too much, people leave. Help too little, people leave.

The AI had to serve two opposite shoppers at once, and know which one it was talking to.

A
The expert

Knows her size perfectly. She just wants the country-conversion math done for her. Ask her one redundant question and she's gone.

B
The learner

Has no idea how this brand fits her body. If we don't teach her, she leaves to hunt for a size chart on another site, and maybe buys there.

Why the obvious fixes had already failed
Static size chartsBuried, generic, and blind to how a specific brand actually cuts.
"Fix the data first"You can't force millions of sellers to relabel years of listings. Waiting for perfect data means waiting forever.
One-size-fits-all pop-upsTreat the expert and the learner the same, and annoy both.
03 · My approach
Signal Strategy, not features

I didn't just design the screens. I designed how the AI behaves.

Instead of a pile of features, I gave the AI one response framework: six moves it can make, picked by context and confidence. Every team, from Search NLP to Checkout, plugged into the same spine.

Educate → Ask → Show → Act → Explain → Learn Ask ↔ Act is the calibration that matters: when to ask a question, and when to just do the work.
The rulebook I wrote for the AI
AI is sure (≥90%)
Act quietly. Apply the size, and pin a receipt bar with a one-tap Undo. No questions, no interruptions. And nothing silent, ever.
AI is unsure (50–89%)
Ask once. Friendly chips like "Which 12?" with the translations printed right on them. Results stay visible behind the question. Never block the screen.
Weak signal (<50%)
Educate, don't filter. Show the full results with size guidance inline. The AI never guesses a filter the shopper didn't give it, so nothing they wanted gets hidden.
Money on the line (any)
Never act. Before any bid or purchase, a human review screen. The AI never taps Buy. That tap belongs to a person. This was our non-negotiable safety invariant.

04 · Design principles
Signal Trust & safety by design

Four rules every screen had to obey

Reduce thinking

Do the conversion math so shoppers never have to. The right size should feel obvious, not calculated.

Build trust

Every AI action leaves a visible receipt with one-tap Undo. Helpful is invisible; trustworthy is visible.

Never act irreversibly

The AI filters, translates, and suggests. Only a human spends money. No exceptions, at any confidence.

Explain on demand

"Why these results?" is always one tap away, and never in the way. Explanations are pulled, not pushed.

05 · The experience
Signal Craft & AI interaction design

A walk through the experience

One search, two shoppers: a first-time shopper the AI must ask about width, and a returning shopper it can confidently act for. Each frame below is the live prototype, opened at that step.

Interactive prototype. Start on Home and walk all eight steps. Switch between First-time and Returning shopper to watch the same search turn from an Ask into an Act.
Open prototype
1

Search: the AI shows its work

You type "size 12 summer dress for a wedding" or "jordans size 11". While the model thinks, the screen streams honest status lines like "Translating UK 12 across sellers…" over shimmering cards. Nobody stares at a dead spinner; the system feels alive and working for you.

Decision: status lines describe real model steps, not theater. When AI is slow, honesty buys patience.
2

The smart choice: ask, or just act

This is the calibration moment. Same framework, two different calls:

The dress → Ask

"12" is genuinely ambiguous, so the AI asks once: a small sheet with translation chips. Results stay visible behind it, so you never lose your place.

Which 12? US 12 UK 12 · a US 8 EU 40
The sneakers → Act

Sneakers default to US sizing by convention, so the AI applies the size without asking. The only question left is the one sneaker buyers actually care about:

Width? Regular Wide Extra wide
3

Results: a steering wheel, not a black box

Every AI action pins a receipt to the top of the results. Tap Edit and you fix the AI instantly. No dead ends, no starting over. A small fit-tolerance control (Exact · ±½ · ±1) recalculates live inventory counts as you drag. "Why these?" explains the reasoning, only when you ask.

Applied UK 12 · translated across 1,204 sellers Edit
4

Product page: measurements, not vibes

Free-text size blurbs became a structured measurement matrix in centimeters. And when a brand runs small, the AI teaches at exactly the right moment: an amber note beside the buy button: "This brand runs about a half size small, so we suggest UK 12 (sized up)."

Decision: educate at the moment of doubt, not in a buried size chart. This is the Educate move, placed where anxiety peaks.
5

Checkout: the last safe moment

Before any money moves, a review sheet shows everything in one place: the item, the size translated side-by-side, the return window, and why the AI chose it. Buying takes an intentional human tap. The AI never taps for you. On timed auctions, a freeze control keeps people safe under countdown pressure.

6

After the purchase: learning, with consent

On the confirmation screen, a quiet receipt slides in: "Saved UK 12 as your dress size · change anytime · Undo." Saving to your profile is a separate, visible choice, never bundled with the purchase. A Size Memory hub in the account shows everything the AI learned, where it learned it, and lets you edit or erase any of it.

Decision: memory you can audit is the difference between "personalized" and "creepy." Consent is a distinct tap, by design.
06 · Leadership
Signal Cross-functional leadership

Four disciplines, one table

Delivering this meant getting Design, Product, Data Science, and Engineering to move as one. Frameworks did the aligning, not meetings.

→

I defined the product strategy with Product and Data Science. We committed to confidence thresholds together, before anyone drew a screen.

→

I created the decision framework. The confidence rulebook meant designers and ML engineers argued from the same table, not from taste.

→

I traded scope deliberately. We shipped honest fallbacks for messy seller data instead of waiting for perfect data that would never come.

→

I designed patterns, not pages. The receipt bar, ask-once chips, safe-moment gate, and memory hub became eBay's reusable agentic UX kit.

Three things we killed, and why
The blocking modal

It punished expert shoppers. Every ask became a sheet that keeps results visible underneath.

Silent personalization

Saving sizes without asking tested as creepy. Learning became a visible, undoable receipt instead.

"Fix the data first"

A multi-year cleanup would have stalled everything. Graceful fallbacks shipped value now.

08 · Reflection
Signal Honest judgment

What I'm taking with me

Trust is a design material.

In testing, shoppers forgave wrong answers when Undo was one tap away. They never forgave silent actions. Reversibility, not accuracy, turned out to be the foundation of trust.

What I'd do differently: align with sellers even earlier.

We brought the Selling team into our global size audit early, and together we shaped structured size capture at listing time. But we treated them as a source of data, not a co-owner of the outcome, so full alignment came weeks later than it should have. Next time, sellers get a seat at the table from day one, and the timeline gets shorter.
