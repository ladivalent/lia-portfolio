01 · The problem
Signal Reframing the problem

Two roles couldn't run a crew.

The Pro site launched in 2018. An account owner could add employees, but only as an Admin or a Purchaser. That works for a two-person shop. It breaks for a contractor with crews on several job sites, all buying on one account.

Call centers filled up with the same request: give owners control over who buys what.

What Pro account owners told us
“I don't trust my worksite employees to make job-related purchases anymore because they buy $500 worth of beef jerky on the company credit card!”
“My employees are purchasing wrong items, I need oversight.”
“My purchasers are buying supplies on the wrong PO/Job Name.”
“My monthly spending is out of control.”
“I want VISIBILITY into what purchasers are buying.”
What was missing
No spending oversight, no granular permissions, and no approval step. Owners had no say before an order went through.
Why it mattered
11,000 legacy IBI customers, acquired in a merger, already relied on an approval workflow, with $86M in yearly Pro revenue tied to it. If the new platform couldn't match it, the migration was at risk.
02 · Who it's for
Signal Systems thinking

Pros aren't equivalent.

New to the space, I started with the Pro Transect, an enterprise UX study that maps Pros from low to high buying complexity. When I laid our complaints over it, they clustered in one place: Growing Local to Multi Regional Pros, at mid-high to high complexity.

How these Pros buy
Role-based responsibilities Approval layers Several buyers per order Purchase management Accurate reconciliation
Why start here
They spend the most, and they're the Pros The Home Depot already serves with advanced, white glove service. Solve their workflow and simpler accounts get it for free.
03 · What research told us
Signal Evidence over opinion

Owners wanted a say before the purchase, not a report after.

With a UX researcher, I ran 15 one-on-one interviews with mid-high complexity Pros, captured How Might We notes, and affinity-mapped them into opportunity areas. In parallel, I compared how four competitors handle account governance.

How might we let owners set up an approval process on their account?
How might we let owners control spending and ensure compliance?
How might we let owners set limits on each user?
How might we make requests and their progress visible?
How might we automate Pros' business processes online?
How might we smooth handoffs between the people involved?

04 · Decision 1 · Strategy
Signal Influence without authority

I pitched workflow as the umbrella for every governance feature.

Other B2B teams were already building CPOs, PO and Job Names, and spend limits, each on its own. I ran a workshop with PMs, designers and developers, shared the research, and showed how those features could live inside one workflow. The room sketched it together, and separate features became one framework.

The governance framework I proposed in the workshop. Each team's feature became a criterion inside workflow.

The bar I set: five things the solution had to do
1Drive complianceOwners create workflows with criteria, and assign each user as a requester or an approver.
2Solve the approver bottleneckSeveral approvers per workflow, and reminders requesters can send on time-sensitive jobs.
3Make every request trackableStatus dashboards and alerts, across email, text and internal notes.
4Automate the routineFew clicks, on desktop and mobile, so approvals keep pace with the job site.
5Stay flexibleOwners can edit a workflow when someone leaves, and people can change a request in flight.
05 · Decision 2 · Structure
Signal Scoping complexity

I split the work into three phases, one per role.

An approval is a handoff between people, so I mapped it by role. With my PM and developers, I scoped the MVP into three phases, then drew a task flow for each, marking every decision point and handoff. The phases kept a squad of six aligned on what we were building next.

PHASE 1
Set up the workflow
Joel, account owner
Creates the workflow, sets its criteria, and assigns requesters and approvers.
PHASE 2
Create and submit a request
Mark, requester
Shops as usual. Orders that meet the criteria go to an approver.
PHASE 3
Review and approve
Kim, approver
Reviews the request, then approves, rejects, or leaves a comment.
Five new touchpoints came out of the flows
Setup and Create Workflow form Manage Workflows dashboard Order request dashboards Order request details Email, text and homepage alerts
06 · Decision 3 · Trade-offs
Signal Judgment under constraints

When the backend set limits, I cut scope, not the promise.

I checked level of effort with engineering as I designed. The backend turned out far less stable than we expected, so we started with the essentials and planned to scale up.

I mapped every feature on value against ease of build to decide what stayed. The rule I held: compromise, but never at the Pro's expense.

08 · Bringing people along
Signal Collaboration

I kept leadership, engineering and Pros in the same loop.

A living deck
Meetings started from the work, not a walkthrough.

To earn buy-in and more resources, I kept a summary deck of the work for the wider team and leadership. I attached it to every meeting invite so partners came prepared, and updated it with each design change. That left the meeting for deciding what to build.

Early technical check-ins
Constraints surfaced while the designs could still change.

I reviewed level of effort with developers as I designed, and a spike settled the editing question. That's how we found the backend limits in time to rescope, instead of after build started.

Testing with Pros changed two things

I wrote and ran usability tests for each phase with Pro candidates, measured by task completion. Testers completed every task. The stumbling points were in the details.

Assigning people meant scrolling a dropdown of 50+ users → A new Autocomplete Multi-Select component Search and pick several people at once, built for accounts with 1,000+ users
Some copy and terms tripped testers up → Three versions of the language, tested with a content strategist Kept the terms Pros recognized
Also heard: Pros liked adding a delegate approver, but weren't sure what a delegate could do, and wanted more than one. That went on the list for the next phase.

10 · Reflection
Signal Honest judgment

Scope is negotiable. Control for the Pro isn't.

What I'd say plainly.

The first release was narrower than the design: one spend limit instead of four criteria, comments instead of edits, and no bulk actions. It solved the core problem and left the rest for later phases.

What I'd do differently.

I'd run the technical spike before blue-sky design, not during it. We found the backend limits while designing, which meant reworking comps mid-stream. Starting from the constraints would have let the first comps ship closer to how they were drawn.

What stuck.

The umbrella idea outlived the feature. Pitching workflow as the home for other teams' work is why it could grow into Projects.

What I'd push for next.

Close the gaps the competitive review showed: approval limits by user, multi-step approvals, and workflows assigned to groups. Amazon Business and Grainger already offered them, and complex Pros run on them.

The lead lesson
Break complex work into phases, check every idea against constraints early, and compromise, but never at the user's expense.
